\begin{abstract}
On a shared high-performance computing cluster, a job is charged for all the
hardware it reserves, used or not. This paper measures the carbon footprint of
one such job, finds which resource carries it, and asks whether an AI-assisted
optimisation of that resource repays its own footprint. The job counts every
31-mer in 5\,000\,000 DNA reads with a single-threaded Python program, on a
whole 128-core node of the Aion cluster, partition \texttt{batch}. A life cycle
assessment built on processor energy counters puts one run at
15~gCO\textsubscript{2}e on that exclusive node. The idle draw of the processors
carries 57\,\% of it, the reserved memory 30\,\% and the program's own work
1.6\,\%. An AI coding agent then changes only what surrounds the unchanged
program: a request of 6~cores and 10~GiB on a shared node of the same
partition, and a Cython build. With the same factors, one run is then
attributed 0.84~g. The assistance and the test runs cost 18.6~g, which the
saving repays in 1.32~runs. For a serial program on a shared cluster, the
allocation, not the code, sets the footprint. A three-step method and a script
carry this check to any batch job.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Computing draws electricity, and the carbon footprint of that electricity
depends on where and how it is generated~\cite{lannelongue2021}. On a shared
high-performance computing (HPC) cluster, a scheduler grants each job an
allocation: nodes, cores and memory, held for a walltime. The hardware a job
holds is attributed to it, whether its program uses that hardware or not. A
request can therefore cost far more than the computation it serves.

The object of this study is one such job. It counts exactly every 31-mer, a
substring of 31 letters of a DNA sequence, in 5\,000\,000 sequencing reads of
the bacterium \emph{Mycobacterium abscessus} (counted on 27~October~2026).
Counting such substrings is a common first step in genome analysis. The
counter, \texttt{kc-py1.py}, is a single-threaded Python program written for
teaching~\cite{kmercnt}. The job runs on Aion, a cluster of the University of
Luxembourg, where it holds a 128-core node exclusively while its program uses
one core.

The scheduler reports no energy figure for this job (Section~\ref{sec:lci}).
Its user sees a walltime and an exit code, but not what the run costs the
climate. The allocation, more than the program, may set that cost. Correcting
the allocation with an artificial intelligence (AI) coding agent has a
footprint of its own, from the AI assistance and the test runs. Such a change is
worth making only if each run saves enough to repay that footprint within the
life of the job.

This study measures the footprint with a life cycle assessment (LCA), the
standard method for the environmental impact of a product over its
life~\cite{iso14040,iso14044}. It counts greenhouse gases in grams of
CO\textsubscript{2} equivalent (gCO\textsubscript{2}e): each gas counts as the
mass of CO\textsubscript{2} that traps as much heat over 100~years, a measure
called the global warming potential over 100~years, or GWP100~\cite{ipcc2021}.

This paper answers the following research question: \emph{what is the GWP100
carbon footprint of one run of an exact 31-mer counting job on an Aion node,
which resource of the job's allocation carries most of it, and how many runs
does an AI-assisted optimisation of that resource need to pay back its own
footprint?} The first two parts concern one particular instance; the third
concerns a means~\cite{shaw2003}.

The paper makes four contributions. The first is a baseline footprint of
15~gCO\textsubscript{2}e per run, built on processor energy counters that an
ordinary user can read. The second is the footprint after an AI-assisted
optimisation that leaves the program unchanged: 0.84~g per run. The third is
the carbon payback of that optimisation, 1.32~runs. The fourth is a three-step
method, with a script, that applies the same check to any batch job. Every
figure traces back to a measured value, a cited factor or a stated assumption,
and the scripts that recompute them are kept with the job.

Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:lca} assesses
the baseline job, from its scope to its hotspots. Section~\ref{sec:optimisation} attacks those hotspots, and
Section~\ref{sec:payback} computes the payback. Section~\ref{sec:threats}
discusses the threats to validity.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
Lannelongue et al.~\cite{lannelongue2021} estimate the footprint of a
computation from its runtime, the cores and memory it uses, the grid intensity
and the power usage effectiveness (PUE) of the data centre, in their Green
Algorithms calculator. This study keeps their memory coefficient but replaces
their processor term with measured energy. Cores held idle still draw power, and
a model that counts only the busy cores misses it
(Section~\ref{sec:interpretation}).

Elsworth et al.~\cite{elsworth2025} measure the energy of AI serving at Google.
Beside the active accelerators, they count the host machines, the idle capacity
held in reserve and the data-centre overhead, which widen the energy of a median
prompt 1.72-fold. Their paper is a preprint, not yet peer reviewed. This study
makes the same argument for one batch job: the capacity it reserves, not the
work it does, carries most of its footprint.

Lakim et al.~\cite{lakim2022} assess a language-model project end to end,
small experiments included, and point to idle hardware as a reason to keep it
fully used. Their count of experiments is why the payback of
Section~\ref{sec:payback} includes the test runs, not only the AI assistance.
Wadenstein and Vanderbauwhede~\cite{wadenstein2025} show that hardware
manufacture dominates the life-cycle emissions of scientific computing centres
once the grid decarbonises, which is why this study keeps a share of hardware
production. Avgerinou et al.~\cite{avgerinou2017} find that the average PUE of
EU data centres declined each year from 2009 to 2016, which is why this study
takes a recent EU average~\cite{eudc2025} as a proxy for Aion.

None of these works separates, for one batch job, the energy of what it
reserves from the energy of what it computes, or asks whether an AI-assisted
change to that reservation repays its own cost.

\section{Life Cycle Assessment}\label{sec:lca}
This section assesses the baseline job in the phases of ISO~14040 and
14044~\cite{iso14040,iso14044}: goal and scope, inventory, impact assessment and
interpretation, followed by the reporting of its hotspots.

\subsection{Goal and scope definition}\label{sec:goalscope}
The goal and scope fix what is studied, the unit every figure refers to, the
boundary of the system and the impact method.

\emph{Goal.} This study quantifies the carbon footprint of one run of the
baseline job. That footprint is the reference for the optimisation of
Section~\ref{sec:optimisation} and its payback in Section~\ref{sec:payback}.

\emph{The job.} The code is \texttt{kc-py1.py} from the public repository
\texttt{lh3/kmer-cnt}~\cite{kmercnt}, frozen at commit
\texttt{e2574719}.\footnote{Full identifiers. Code: \texttt{lh3/kmer-cnt}
commit \texttt{e2574719cfb7\allowbreak 84915d80eb58\allowbreak
28e78dfae4cf\allowbreak dd7b}. Input SHA-256:
\texttt{dd2ef49aebab\allowbreak fed1574b55fd\allowbreak
002025f29b2f\allowbreak da88c70b2095\allowbreak f5e1b3a822ce\allowbreak e736}.
Output SHA-256: \texttt{06a32de77200\allowbreak 121a397050e0\allowbreak
15a257953dc9\allowbreak 56b7854027a1\allowbreak ed73b15afc12\allowbreak bf42}.
Job script: project repository commit \texttt{253d93ec726b\allowbreak
82670c8f4b84\allowbreak da20e9528c9a\allowbreak 59ef}.}
It reads DNA reads from standard input in FASTA, a plain-text format for
sequences. DNA has two strands, and a 31-mer on one strand appears on the other
as its reverse complement. The program therefore counts each 31-mer under the
alphabetically smaller of its two forms, called the canonical 31-mer, and skips
every 31-mer that contains \texttt{N}, the letter for an unread base. It prints
a histogram of 255 lines: line~$n$ gives the number of distinct canonical
31-mers that occur $n$ times, and line~255 also holds those that occur more
often. The input is a compressed file of 187\,693\,955~bytes from the
repository's v0.1 release~\cite{kmercnt}. The program converts lower-case bases
to upper case in the last read only, so a lower-case base elsewhere would change
the count. No read of this input holds one (checked on 27~October~2026). Every
run writes the same 1\,972-byte histogram. The footnote identifies the code,
the input and the histogram, the last two by their SHA-256 checksums,
fingerprints of their exact content.

\emph{Repository.} The job, its optimised versions, the measurement records,
the calculation scripts and the tool of Section~\ref{sec:generalises} are in
the public repository \repourl, at commit \texttt{\repocommit}.

\emph{The machine.} The job runs in the \texttt{batch} partition of Aion, the
queue for production jobs, at the \texttt{normal} quality-of-service level. A
regular Aion node holds two AMD EPYC~7H12 processors of 64~cores each, 256~GB of
memory, a 480~GB local solid-state disk (SSD) and one 100~Gb/s InfiniBand
network port~\cite{ulhpcaion}. The software is Python~3.11.5 from the cluster's
default release, as recorded in every run's log.

\emph{Assumption of equal nodes.} Slurm, the scheduler, places each run on
whichever node is free, so the runs of this study fall on several regular
nodes. The study assumes that every regular node of the partition gives the same
performance and the same power draw for the same request. Under this
assumption, runs on different nodes measure the same job. All these nodes are of
one type, but the assumption is not proven: Section~\ref{sec:threats} weighs
it.

\emph{Allocation and use.} Each baseline run holds one node exclusively:
128~cores, all the memory Slurm can reserve (224\,000~MiB, where 1~MiB is
$2^{20}$~bytes) and a 30-minute limit. Its program uses one of those cores. The
author submits it from a login node, with a standard student account. Five runs
are measured, and the job is assumed to run 100~times per year
(Section~\ref{sec:threats}).

\emph{Functional unit.} One run of \texttt{kc-py1.py} at commit
\texttt{e2574719} that counts the exact canonical 31-mers of the fixed input and
writes the reference histogram, submitted by a student user to partition
\texttt{batch} of Aion and executed on one regular node. Its impact is reported
in gCO\textsubscript{2}e at GWP100. The allocation is deliberately not part of
the unit, so that a change of allocation (Section~\ref{sec:optimisation}) is
compared on the same unit.

\emph{Method.} One impact category, climate change, characterised with the
IPCC~2021 method of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate
Change~\cite{ipcc2021}, as GWP100.

\emph{Boundary.} Four components are inside the boundary:
(1)~the \emph{compute node}, with its electricity during the walltime and a
share of its manufacture;
(2)~the \emph{interconnect}, the InfiniBand network that carries the job's
reads and writes;
(3)~the \emph{storage}, the shared General Parallel File System (GPFS) that
holds the input, the code and the output;
(4)~the \emph{site}, the cooling and power distribution around the machine,
which enters through the PUE.
Outside are the author's laptop, the login node, the network to the cluster,
the download of the input, the development of the code and every node the
allocation does not reserve. For the node, production and use are kept; for the
other three components, use only. End of life is not inventoried, because the
disposal route of Aion's hardware is not published.

\subsection{Life cycle inventory}\label{sec:lci}
The inventory lists the physical flows the assessment rests on and how each is
obtained. Table~\ref{tab:inventory} in Appendix~A gives every entry with its
source and its reading date.

\emph{Time and resources.} The job runs five times on 29~September~2026, one
run after another on the same node. All five end without error and write the
same histogram. Their walltimes span 362 to 372~s, with a median of 368~s. The
scheduler's accounting records give the cores held, the memory reserved and the
peak memory the program uses.

\emph{Energy.} The scheduler's energy fields are empty for every run, which is
missing data, not zero energy. The processors (CPUs), however, carry energy
counters, called Running Average Power Limit (RAPL), that an ordinary user can
read. Five more runs on the same day read them before and after the counting
step, on a second regular node because the first is busy. The two processor
packages draw a median of 67\,834~J per run (range 67\,129 to 68\,827~J), or
188~W on average. Read for 60~s after the fifth run, the idle node draws 183~W.
The program's own activity therefore adds only 1\,863~J per run (median): 97\,\%
of the package energy is drawn whether the program runs or not. RAPL covers the
processor packages only; memory, board, disk and network card fall outside it.

\emph{Models.} The Green Algorithms model~\cite{lannelongue2021} gives a second
estimate: the walltime times the power of the cores and memory held, with
280~W per 64-core processor at its rated power~\cite{ulhpcaion} and 0.3725~W per
GB of memory. Counting one core gives 36.7~kJ per run; counting all 128~cores at
rated power gives 241~kJ. The measured packages alone, 67.8~kJ, draw almost
twice the one-core figure, so both model figures serve as scenarios, not as
bounds.

\emph{Compute node.} The job reserves 224\,000~MiB and touches 7.40~GiB of it,
3.4\,\%. For manufacture, the ecoinvent~3.11 database~\cite{ecoinvent311} holds
no server dataset (searched on 29~September~2026). Its closest dataset, a 2002
desktop computer without screen, carries 221~kgCO\textsubscript{2}e. A two-socket
AMD EPYC server, the Dell PowerEdge R7525, has a published production footprint
of 1\,768.4~kgCO\textsubscript{2}e, of which 854.7~kg comes from two 4~TB
SSDs~\cite{dellr7525}. Neither describes an Aion node; Section~\ref{sec:lcia}
chooses between them. Aion has run since late 2021 and has no published
retirement date~\cite{ulhpcaion}, so a six-year service life is assumed. A
shared item is divided by the node-seconds a run holds over the node-seconds the
node delivers in its life: 368~s of 189\,345\,600~s, a share of
$1.94\times10^{-6}$.

\emph{Interconnect and storage.} The job runs on one node, so its only network
traffic is file input and output. It reads about 188~MB of compressed input and
19~MiB of software, and writes the 1\,972-byte histogram, as inferred from the
scheduler's disk counters. Neither the network nor the file system meters its
energy per job, so both are left out of the result.

\emph{Site.} The PUE is the ratio of the electricity a whole data centre draws
to the electricity its computing equipment draws. The centre publishes none for
Aion, so the study declares 1.36, the energy-weighted average of 681 EU data centres for
2023~\cite{eudc2025}, read on 29~September~2026. This PUE adds cooling and power
distribution to the counted node energy. It cannot supply the computing energy
of the network and storage that is left out.

\subsection{Life cycle impact assessment}\label{sec:lcia}
The impact assessment multiplies each flow by a characterisation factor, an
impact per unit of flow. Table~\ref{tab:chain} in Appendix~A gives the chain,
which a script of the project repository recomputes from the raw records.

\emph{Node energy.} The measured packages cover the counting step only, while
the job holds the whole node for its walltime. The node estimate therefore adds
three terms per run: the measured package energy; the idle rate over the 1 to
3~s of the allocation outside the counting step; and the memory model,
0.3725~W/GB for all 256~GB, or 95.4~W, over the walltime. Its median is
103\,146~J (0.0287~kWh, range 101\,622 to 104\,215~J) over 364~s, the median
walltime of the same runs.

\emph{Site and grid.} The PUE of 1.36 raises this to 0.0390~kWh drawn by the
site. The grid factor is 337~gCO\textsubscript{2}e/kWh: ecoinvent~3.11, market
for electricity, low voltage, Luxembourg, 2020--2024, IPCC~2021
GWP100~\cite{ecoinventlu}, read on 19~October~2026. It describes the
electricity consumed in Luxembourg, imports and grid losses included, which is
the electricity the site draws. A factor for national generation alone would
leave out the imports. The operational impact is
$0.0390\times337=13.1$~gCO\textsubscript{2}e per run.

\emph{Hardware.} The study uses the R7525 figure, because the ecoinvent desktop
models a single-processor machine of 11.3~kg from 2002. An Aion node holds one
480~GB SSD, not two 4~TB SSDs, so those are removed. This leaves
913.7~kgCO\textsubscript{2}e, a proxy for a node, not its footprint. The Dell
study uses IPCC~2013 and publishes no emissions per gas, so the figure cannot
be converted to IPCC~2021: it is kept and labelled (Section~\ref{sec:threats}).
The grid factor is not applied to it, because the hardware is made worldwide.
One run holds $364/189\,345\,600=1.92\times10^{-6}$ of a six-year life, so it
carries 1.76~gCO\textsubscript{2}e.

\emph{Result.} One run of the baseline job emits
$13.1+1.8\approx15$~gCO\textsubscript{2}e.

\subsection{Interpretation}\label{sec:interpretation}
\emph{Against daily life.} A 5~W LED bulb lit for one hour emits
1.7~gCO\textsubscript{2}e on the same grid factor, so one run equals about
9~hours of light. It also equals about 50~m in a small petrol car, at
291~gCO\textsubscript{2}e per km~\cite{ries2026s2}, a cradle-to-grave figure
with a wider boundary. At 100~runs per year, the job emits
1.5~kgCO\textsubscript{2}e per year.

\emph{Against the scenarios.} Table~\ref{tab:scenarios} in Appendix~A sets the
chosen estimate beside the alternatives, all on the same 364~s. The measured
packages alone fall 34\,\% short, because they lack the memory. The one-core
model falls almost threefold short. It gives the processors 4.4~W where they
measure 188~W, because it gives the 127 unused cores no power. The all-cores
model, at the rated 560~W, overshoots by 135~kJ. The measured 188~W is 34\,\% of
that rating, a plausible share for a node on which one core of 128 works. The
program's own work above idle, 1.9~kJ, is less than 2\,\% of the node estimate.
A user who counts only the cores a program uses therefore understates the
footprint of an exclusive job, which is why the chosen result keeps the
measurement wherever one exists.

\subsection{Reporting}\label{sec:reporting}
The baseline footprint is 15~gCO\textsubscript{2}e per run: 13.1~g of
electricity and 1.8~g of hardware production, for one run that holds an
exclusive Aion node for 364~s. It rests on IPCC~2021 GWP100, measured processor
energy, modelled memory, a PUE of 1.36, 337~gCO\textsubscript{2}e/kWh and a
six-year hardware life. Across the scenarios of Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}, it
ranges from 6.4 to 32~g. It scales with the node-time held, not with the work
done.

Two hotspots carry the result, and both come from the allocation rather than
from the counting. The idle draw of the processors takes 8.5~g, 57\,\% of the
total with its share of the site overhead, while the program's own work takes
1.6\,\%. The reserved memory takes 4.4~g, 30\,\%, and the job touches 3.4\,\% of
it. Hardware production takes the remaining 12\,\%.

\section{Optimisation}\label{sec:optimisation}

\subsection{Optimisation procedure}\label{sec:optproc}
The hotspots come from what the job holds, not from what it computes. The
optimisation therefore changes what surrounds the program and leaves the
program itself unchanged, byte for byte, so that the functional unit holds by
construction. It uses two levers, in this order.
(1)~\emph{Allocation.} Request only the cores and memory the program uses, on a
node shared with other jobs. The job is then charged its share of the node's
idle draw, memory and production, not all of it.
(2)~\emph{Execution.} Run the same source faster, by compiling it with Cython,
a compiler that turns Python code into C, so that the request is held for less
time. This lever comes second, because the program's own work is only 1.6\,\% of
the baseline.

Before and after keep the program, the input, the partition, the node type,
the protocol (five consecutive runs, median) and the factors. They run on
different nodes, so the comparison rests on the assumption of equal nodes of
Section~\ref{sec:goalscope}.

An AI coding agent makes each change: OpenCode, with the DeepSeek V4 Flash model
the course provides. The author chooses each lever, writes the agent's
instructions, which forbid any change to the program, and checks every change.
The agent's configuration fixes the model, a low reasoning effort, a limit on
steps and the commands it may run. With a short project instruction file, it
cuts the fixed cost of one request from 12\,365 to 5\,920 input tokens.

\subsection{Implementation}\label{sec:implementation}
\emph{Allocation.} The new job script requests one task with 6~cores, 10~GiB of
memory, no exclusive node and a 10-minute limit. The memory is 1.25 times the
measured 7.40~GiB peak, rounded up. Aion grants at most 1\,750~MB of memory per
core (read on 27~October~2026), so 10~GiB needs 6~cores, and the program still
runs on one of them. This rule comes from a failed attempt: a request of one
core and 8~GB made the scheduler raise the cores silently, and the job then
failed to start.

\emph{Compilation.} A short build job compiles the unchanged source with
Cython~3.0.10 into a standalone program, against the Python of the same
software release. The build log records the checksum of the compiled source,
which proves it is the frozen file. Without type annotations, Cython removes
only the cost of interpreting Python, so a modest gain is expected. The
compiled program runs with the same request.

\emph{Rejected.} An earlier attempt by the agent, outside this scope, rewrites
the counter around 62-bit integers. A rewrite changes the program, so
it is discarded and left out of the payback (Section~\ref{sec:threats}).

\emph{Check.} Both versions write the frozen counter's histogram on a sample of
100\,000 reads, and every measured run writes the reference histogram on the
full input. The program, its output and the functional unit are unchanged.

\subsection{Footprint after optimisation}\label{sec:afteropt}
Each version runs five times on 10~November~2026, alternating, on regular nodes
shared with other jobs. Each run holds 6~cores and 10~GiB and peaks at 7.40~GiB,
as before. The right-sized script takes a median of 367~s (362 to 377~s),
against 364~s on the exclusive node: sharing the node does not slow the
program. The compiled program takes 340~s (335 to 351~s), 7.4\,\% less.

On a shared node, the processor counters see every job, so this job's energy is
attributed rather than measured. Each run is charged 6/128 of the node's idle
draw, the program's own work at 5.2~W per busy core from the baseline
measurement, and the memory model on the 10.74~GB reserved. Production is shared
by the larger of the core and memory shares. Applied to the whole node, the same
chain returns the baseline within 0.1\,\%.

The right-sized script is attributed 0.91~gCO\textsubscript{2}e per run, and
the compiled program 0.84~g (range 0.83 to 0.87~g), which is kept. An optimised
run thus carries 6\,\% of the baseline, an estimated saving of 14.0~g per run.
This is a smaller footprint charged to the job, under the assumption that other
jobs use what it frees, not a measured fall in the cluster's emissions. The node
energy charged to a run falls from 103.1 to 6.0~kJ. The idle share still leads,
with 44\,\% of the new total, ahead of the program's own work (27\,\%), memory
(20\,\%) and production (9\,\%). Table~\ref{tab:afteropt} in Appendix~A sets
the three versions side by side. Of the 14.04~g saved, the allocation brings
13.98~g and compilation 0.07~g.

\subsection{What generalises}\label{sec:generalises}
\emph{1.~The method.} Any batch job can be checked in three steps.
(1)~\emph{Recognise.} Compare what the job holds with what it uses, from the
scheduler's records: the cores held against the CPU time over the walltime, and
the memory reserved against the peak memory. A ratio far below one is the
hotspot of this paper.
(2)~\emph{Change.} Request one task with the cores the program keeps busy and
1.25 times its peak memory. Raise the cores if the cluster limits the memory per
core, so that the scheduler does not raise them silently. Drop exclusive access.
Only then shorten the walltime without touching the program, for example by
compiling it, and check that the gain repays the extra work.
(3)~\emph{Check.} The output must be byte-identical, the job must complete
within its new limits over five runs, and the footprint per run, with the same
factors, must fall.
The method does not help where a job already keeps its cores busy, where a
cluster grants only whole nodes, or where the freed resources stay idle.

\emph{2.~The tool.} \texttt{allocation-footprint.py}, written by a second
agent, applies the method to any finished job. From a job identifier on a
cluster, or from a saved accounting record anywhere else, it prints what the job
held and used, the footprint per run of that allocation and of the right-sized
one, and the request lines to use. Every factor is an option, with this study's
values as defaults. It needs Python~3.6 or newer and no other package, and it
sits in \texttt{job/tool/} of the repository named in
Section~\ref{sec:goalscope}, with its own README.
It passes three checks. First, its inputs and outputs are those just listed.
Second, it runs on jobs other than the one it is written for. It reproduces
14.88~g on a baseline record and 0.92 and 0.84~g on optimised runs, and on an
unrelated 61-s measurement job it reports 2.45~g for 0.7~CPU-seconds and
recommends one core and 1~GiB. Third, it stops with a clear message on a record
that lacks the allocation fields and on a job that does not complete. On the
optimised job, it recommends the request already in use: nothing is left to
cut.

\section{Carbon Payback}\label{sec:payback}
The payback divides the cost of producing the optimisation by the saving per
run. Only the path that leads to the kept optimisation is counted;
Section~\ref{sec:threats} gives the weight of the discarded rewrite.
Table~\ref{tab:payback} in Appendix~A details both terms.

\emph{Cost.} The AI assistance takes ten agent sessions, 62 requests and
27\,924 output tokens, reasoning included. The course factor of 1.03~Wh per
1\,000 output tokens~\cite{ries2026s8}, from one query scenario of Oviedo et
al.~\cite{oviedo2026}, gives 0.029~kWh. As the course prescribes, the grid
factor of Section~\ref{sec:lcia} converts it to 9.7~gCO\textsubscript{2}e. Where
the model runs is not known, so that factor is an assumption for the AI service.
The runs on Aion cost 8.87~g: 8.81~g for the ten measured runs and 0.06~g for
twenty short jobs: failed launches, builds, sample tests and accounting. The total cost is
18.6~gCO\textsubscript{2}e. Tests on the author's laptop are outside the
boundary.

\emph{Payback.} The saving is $14.888-0.844=14.04$~gCO\textsubscript{2}e per
run, so the payback is $18.6/14.04=1.32$~runs: the second run after the change
repays it. At the assumed 100~runs per year, that takes about five days. The AI
assistance accounts for 0.69 of these runs and the runs on Aion for 0.63.
Compilation alone repays its own 7.3~g only after 109~runs, about 13~months,
because it saves just 0.067~g per run. The general-purpose assistant that helped
write this paper (Appendix~B) is not counted, because it does not produce the
optimisation.

\subsection{What this means}
One run of this job emits 15~gCO\textsubscript{2}e on an exclusive Aion node,
more than half of it from cores held idle. Without a change to the program,
right-sizing and compilation cut the footprint attributed to one run to
0.84~g, and they repay their own 19~g within two runs. The lesson reaches
beyond this job. For a serial program on a shared cluster, the allocation, not
the code, sets the footprint. Correcting it pays back fast, because each run
then saves almost all of its footprint. Making the code faster pays back slowly
once the allocation is right. The next steps are to run the tool on other users'
jobs, and to confirm the attributed energy with a measurement on a node that
holds only the new request.

\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}
This section gathers the limitations of Sections~\ref{sec:lcia}
to~\ref{sec:payback}. For each, it states whether it makes the baseline of
15~gCO\textsubscript{2}e, the optimised 0.84~g or the payback of 1.32~runs too
high or too low and, where possible, by how much. Each shift moves one input of
the calculation scripts and holds the others fixed.

\emph{Energy that is not measured.} No instrument reaches the whole node. The
board, the local SSD, the network card and power conversion are not counted, so
the result is too low. Each 50~W they draw adds 2.3~g per run, or 16\,\%. The
memory term is a generic coefficient~\cite{lannelongue2021} and can err in
either direction. The idle rate is a single 60-s reading, so the 57\,\% hotspot
share moves with any change in idle draw over time. The energy of the network
and the storage is left out, so the result is too low by an amount Aion does not
publish.

\emph{Proxy factors.} The PUE of 1.36 is an EU average, not a site value, and
Aion's cold-plate cooling~\cite{ulhpcaion} may put the site below it. A PUE of
1.1 gives 12.4~g (17\,\% less), and 1.6 gives 17.2~g (16\,\% more). The grid
factor describes low-voltage supply. If the site draws at a higher voltage, part
of the grid losses does not apply, and the result is slightly too high. The
dataset's description mentions high voltage although its title names low
voltage; the study follows the title. The hardware proxy is a rack server,
while an Aion node is one of three nodes in a blade~\cite{ulhpcaion}. The
ecoinvent desktop would give 13.6~g (9\,\% less), and the R7525 with its SSDs
16.5~g (11\,\% more). The node's own SSD, the chassis, the switches, the storage
servers and the building are left out, so the result is too low. The R7525
figure uses IPCC~2013, whose GWP100 for methane and N\textsubscript{2}O differs
from IPCC~2021 by 3\,\% or less~\cite{myhre2013,ipcc2021}. Each 10\,\% change on
the hardware term moves the total by 1.2\,\%. A four-year life gives 15.8~g
(6\,\% more), and an eight-year life 14.4~g (3\,\% less). Downtime spreads
production over fewer node-seconds, so the hardware term is too low by an
unknown fraction.

\emph{Cache state.} File caches are neither reset nor measured. The counting
step is bound by computation, with a CPU time of 99.8\,\% of its walltime, so a
cache effect is bounded by the 2.5\,\% spread of the node estimates.

\emph{Equal nodes.} The study relies on the assumption of equal regular nodes
(Section~\ref{sec:goalscope}) at three points. The baseline timing and energy
come from two different nodes, the optimised runs fall on two others, and one
idle reading serves every node. The runs bound the error. The baseline median
is 368~s on one node and 364~s on the other; with the first, the result would be
15.05 instead of 14.89~g, 1.1\,\% more. An idle reading on a third node gives
178.6~W against 182.6~W, 2\,\% apart, which moves the idle share by the same
fraction. In the optimised series, the runs on the second node differ from those
on the first by less than 2\,\%, in opposite directions for the two versions.
That is less than the 4\,\% spread of the runs on one node alone. The data
therefore do not contradict the assumption, but two runs cannot prove it. A node
2\,\% slower moves the optimised result by about 2\,\% and the payback by less
than 0.03~runs. The 7.4\,\% gain of compilation exceeds these differences, and
the alternating runs put both versions on the same nodes.

\emph{Attributed energy after optimisation.} The optimised energy is
attributed, not measured. Its program term reuses the baseline's 5.2~W per busy
core, although the compiled program may draw a different power. That term is
27\,\% of the optimised result, so an error of 20\,\% on it moves the result by
0.05~g and the saving by less than 0.4\,\%. The saving also assumes that other
jobs use the 122~cores and the memory this job frees; 132 regular nodes are
shared between jobs on 27~October~2026. If the freed cores stayed idle, each run
would bear the whole node's idle draw: the saving would fall to 6.5~g and the
payback would rise to 2.9~runs.

\emph{Cost of the AI assistance.} The token factor is a ratio from one
short-query scenario, 500 input and 300 output tokens to a model of more than
200~billion parameters~\cite{oviedo2026}, not a measurement of these sessions.
Agent sessions re-send their context: these read about 11\,500 input tokens per
request, and very long inputs raise the energy of reading them
substantially~\cite{oviedo2026}. The model is a mixture of experts, with
13~billion of its 284~billion parameters active per token~\cite{openrouterdsv4},
on hardware that is not published. If the assistance cost ten times the
estimate, the payback would be 7.5~runs, about four weeks. The requests reach
one of three providers whose location the session records do not give. Each
100~gCO\textsubscript{2}e/kWh more on their grid adds 0.20~runs to the payback,
and twice the Luxembourg factor gives 2.0~runs.

\emph{Discarded path.} The agent's discarded rewrite of the counter cost
10.1~g: 7.1~g of AI assistance and 3.0~g of runs. Counted, it would raise the
payback from 1.32 to 2.04~runs.

\emph{Runs per year.} The figure of 100~runs per year is an assumption. It
changes the annual footprint and the payback period, not the result per run.

\emph{Scope.} The result covers climate change only, for one job, and hides the
mineral and water use of hardware production. End of life is outside it, so it
is too low by the share of disposal, while the ecoinvent desktop proxy includes
packaging and disposal. The car comparator is cradle to grave, which makes one
run look smaller beside it. A change that frees the node may lead the job to run
more often. The result supports no comparison with another job.
